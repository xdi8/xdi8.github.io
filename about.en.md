## The language of an individual

Shidinn was born in the 1990s, first under the name “New Language”, later “Xiding” (西丁语), and finally renamed Shidinn (希顶语) in 2018. Back then, typing Chinese characters into a computer was far less convenient than it is today; a Chinese written in letters could be entered and spread as easily as English. Huang hoped this would “alphabetize” Chinese, perhaps even turn Shidinn into an international language that foreigners could learn more easily. At the same time, Mandarin is full of homophones — and in dialects that do not even distinguish the initials *n* and *l*, homophony is worse. A language without homophones, he believed, would spare readers and writers many mistakes. Those are the twin aims of Shidinn: alphabetizing Chinese, and deprecating homophones.

Huang’s own life, however, was not smooth. Most of the letters came from inspiration, and his manuscripts were often written in sorrow. He once hoped Shidinn would win official support, and travelled to Beijing twice, sleeping rough on its streets, but both attempts failed. With no road left, he turned to the public. In January 2020, he asked on Zhihu:

> I created the Shidinn letters, and ever since, disasters have not ceased. Have I angered the gods, and earned this sorrowful life? Should I destroy the Shidinn letters?

A month later, in February 2020, Raymond’s article “Unmasking Shidinn” (《揭秘「希顶语」》) brought this private language into the public eye for the first time, igniting a wave of “developing Shidinn” and “adding bricks to the Shidinn tower”.

## How letters “reimagine” Chinese

At its heart, Shidinn is about the “word sound” of Chinese characters. Its 45 basic letters carry both meaning and phonemes: each character is built from zero or more meaning-carrying letters (like radicals) plus one main phonetic letter, its “sound stem”. The radical-like letters are read by their letter names, while the phonetic stem follows a constructed phonology — based mainly on Southwestern Mandarin, possibly blended with some Hunanese. To keep initials and finals fitting neatly, a word’s sound does not always match Standard Mandarin — so in theory, every Chinese character can be given one definite pronunciation, and no two characters need ever sound alike.

Today Shidinn can transcribe modern Chinese character by character, and it has also grown poems, essays and songs of its own.

## An unfinished language

Shidinn remains an unfinished constructed language: its sounds and vocabulary are fairly settled, but its grammar has long lacked an agreed definition. Huang Quefei once drafted a grammar, but the materials were lost; his later self-authored texts are mostly character-by-character transcriptions of Chinese in Shidinn sounds. On this basis the community has developed dialects with grammars of their own — Standard Shidinn, Common Shidinn, Xizhi (希致语) and others — while some dialects do not even use Chinese characters as an intermediary. The community is now working to standardize the language, so that Shidinn may finally have a unified standard.

## From one lamp to a thousand stars

After Shidinn became known online, many people began adding their own bricks to the tower:

- In February 2020, Raymond’s article “Unmasking Shidinn” drew the attention of linguistics enthusiasts and scholars;
- In September 2020, the first Shidinn font, Gentium Xdi8 (based on the open-source Gentium Plus), was released;
- The Shidinn QQ group, the journal *Shidinnological Letters* (《希顶学通讯》), and the community’s Bilibili channel followed;
- In January 2021, the Shidinn Wiki went online, becoming a home for knowledge and reference.

Today a distinctive culture has taken shape — lantern riddles, essays, poems, novels and songs written in Shidinn — while many conlangers build new dialects upon it, and tools from fonts and translators to input methods and even operating systems keep appearing. No sovereign state has granted Shidinn official status, yet the work of researching and spreading it continues.
